## The tiers, restated

**Reader (free).** Everything on the page, including the Navigator, the Season and the Horizon, because the sources are public and the value of the terminal is that it is read. Free is the funnel: the page is on the owner’s résumé and in the hands of the people he writes to.

**Private (on request; the number to test is $40–60 a month, or $400 a year).** One reader, one desk. Adds: the Brief by e-mail after each refresh; the Navigator with the reader’s own cases saved and a printed plan; the Season with the room’s “going” list and the opt-in contact lines; the Horizon’s theses as a quarterly letter; priority corrections. The buyer is a collector, a junior at a house or a gallery, a student of the field.

**Desk (on request; the number to test is $2,500–5,000 a year a seat, or a flat $15,000 for a house).** Adds: the data as CSV and JSON after each refresh (lots, houses, hubs, regimes, segments, the calendar, the events, the Navigator’s rules); custom lenses; an analyst’s hour each month; a Navigator route checked by a named specialist (shipper or lawyer) under a referral agreement; early access. The buyer is an advisory firm, a family office, a house’s private-sales desk, a logistics firm that wants its clients to arrive informed.

## Three further lines, in order of effort

1. **The Navigator as a referral desk.** Every plotted route ends in a professional’s quote: a shipper, a broker, an insurer, a lawyer. A referral fee on the introduction (the shippers and insurers pay these routinely) turns the free tool into income without charging the reader. Needs: two or three named firms per corridor, a disclosure line on the page, and a form that sends the plan (never the reader’s identity without consent).
2. **The Season as a ticket and travel partner.** The organiser’s ticket page is linked already; affiliate links where fairs offer them, and a “two weeks in the market” itinerary service for Desk members (the owner’s own planning, sold as a service).
3. **The data as an API.** The Desk tier’s CSV and JSON, served as a versioned endpoint with a key, for the firms that want to put the sale room, the regimes and the Navigator’s rules into their own systems. Price by seat and by call volume; the live desk already refreshes the files on a schedule.

## What stays free, and why

The sources, the flags and the worked cases stay free because they are the proof. A Navigator that hid its law behind a paywall would be a worse product and a weaker résumé line. What is sold is the reader’s own state (saved routes, the Brief, the room), the data in bulk, and the owner’s time.

**1. **3. The Transformation as a pre-read for compliance.** A bank’s AML officer, a house’s compliance desk and a broker’s onboarding team each answer the same question — where does this money come from, where does it go, which control catches it — before a professional opinion is sought. The Transformation gives the first answer in a minute, with the instrument named at each gate and the lawful version when a gate closes. It is a reading, not advice: the page says so, names the controls, and never draws a way around them; that is what makes it sellable to the regulated side rather than the other side. Price to test: inside the Private tier for an individual ($40–60 a month), inside Desk for a firm; the worked cases stay free as the proof.

**4. The Navigator with the money’s road.** The Navigator now closes a route when the buyer’s money cannot lawfully leave its country, and names the lawful version. For a shipper or a lawyer who wants a client to arrive informed, that is the referral desk of part two with one more gate.

## The legal position, stated plainly

The terminal describes the law as the sources state it, flags what it could not confirm, and gives no advice. Every gate links its instrument; the Transformation and the Navigator refuse to plot around a closed gate and say why; the typologies and cases are the regulators’ own. The three lines to keep on the page: “a compliance reading, not advice”; “every figure sourced and flagged”; “the precise answer comes from a named professional”. Under that framing the product sits with the regulated side (banks, houses, brokers, shippers, lawyers), which is the buyer with a budget. A version that hid its sources or promised an outcome would be both a worse product and a liability.

## 2. Investing in art today: how would that go?

The honest answer the evidence gives, and the page gives it with the sources on every line. Five tides carry the full weight of the evidence: the trophy tide (sales above US$10m up 30% in 2025 and US works above US$10m up nearly 40%, Art Basel/UBS; the Big Three at US$6.8bn in the first half of 2026, up 70%, ArtTactic; the Pollock at US$181.2m and the Brancusi at US$107.6m in May), the estate tide (Newhouse US$630.8m, Lauder US$527.5m, Lewis US$406.2m, Mnuchin US$173m: the recovery is inheritance-driven, and Deloitte’s US$992bn of art and collectibles passing in a decade says it continues), the Impressionist and Modern tide (auction sales up 47% in 2025), the Old Masters tide (up 30% to just under US$1.2bn, with a Michelangelo at US$27.2m and a Rembrandt drawing at US$17.8m: a supply-and-provenance market) and the American tide. The owner’s own example holds at strength four: sculpture took the trophy slots (the Brancusi, Moore’s £26.3m record, the Matisse bronzes at US$49m, the £13.6m Laocoön) and design crossed into them (two Lalanne records, US$31.4m and US$33.45m, four months apart) — and the risks sit in the same card: the US$70m Giacometti that failed in May 2025 and the Rodin that passed in May 2026.

## 3. The capture mechanism: five things a house cannot build for itself

An auction house knows its own clients, its own consignments, its own guarantees and its own compliance files, and it cannot publish any of it. The value the ocean captures is precisely what sits outside a single house’s walls — and it is captured at the moment a desk acts.

1. **The calls ledger.** Every prediction and rumour on the page is dated, attributed, labelled and resolved where the outcome is known. The record shows whose whispers come true: Puck teased a Sotheby’s sale ‘expected to clear nearly half a billion’ (7 July 2026); Katya Kazakina reported a US$450m guarantee ‘per her sources’ (Artnet, relayed 14 August); Sotheby’s confirmed the guaranteed twelve-painting Blaquier-Arrieta sale ‘in the region of $450m’ on 30 September, for 18 November. The forecasters who called a weak 2026 top end were wrong (Christie’s +71%, the Big Three US$6.8bn in the half); Horowitz, HENI and Citi’s Bickar read it right; Maneker admits the Mnuchin call he missed and now calls ‘the takeoff phase of a new auction cycle’. A house cannot keep a public scorecard of the trade’s forecasters, least of all its own specialists. A terminal can, and a scorecard is the only honest way to sell foresight: the reader sees the record before the next call. *Captured at:* the morning read, every season (the seat).
2. **The consignment radar.** The estate tide says where the next trophy sales come from; the collectors file says where the collections sit, in which vehicle, with which advisers, and what each sold at the top since 2020. ## 4. Where does our leverage lie?

Seven places, as the record states them. **The structured database is a commodity; the record of what is not in the results is not.** Artprice (30m results, 7,200 houses), Artnet (1,800 houses from 1985), LiveArt (10m records), Masterworks (50m records) all rebuild the same auction results; none holds the guarantee terms, the private price, the underbidder, the condition, or the regulatory road. **The regulatory map nobody sells.** HMRC’s own guidance (updated 6 August 2024) draws the line: a business that gives art advice, charges for research or provenance checks without taking part in the transaction, or introduces customers, need not register as an art market participant; one that negotiates a price or acts for a customer at the threshold and above must (£10,000 since SI 2026/621 of June 2026; €10,000 before, as the house research found on legislation.gov.uk). A terminal that reads and never deals stays on the research side of that line — and the FCA does not regulate buying, selling or advising on art (as The Art Newspaper quoted a lawyer in June 2024; fractional interests are another matter). **The trust gap is measured.** Only 38% of collectors and 27% of wealth managers report high trust in art-market data (Deloitte/ArtTactic 2025, as Paperjam reports it); more than half name the lack of it as the obstacle. A scorecard is the answer to a trust gap. **The AI position in 2026.** The houses use the models for translation, categorisation and personalisation (The Art Newspaper’s 2024 survey); LiveArt sells an XGBoost estimate with an ±11% band; the trade’s own limit, stated by the new entrants, is that more than 60% of sales are private and the models cannot see condition or provenance and invent paperwork. The terminal’s answer is a reading with the source on every line, not a model’s guess. **The cost structure.** Sotheby’s carried 2,218 staff, US$813m of commissions and fees, a US$248m pretax loss and US$2.76bn of net debt in 2024 (its filings as reported), and US$363m of adjusted EBITDA on US$1.4bn of revenue in 2025; Artnet lost money every quarter before its sale; a desk of three runs on a fraction. **Two-sided data.** The Baer Faxt’s thirty years of buyer and underbidder identities came from one editor and informants in the saleroom, not from a licence — over 10,000 works by 2025. The trade entering what it sees is the only data the houses cannot copy. **The price the trade already pays.** ArtTactic at £195 a month; the Baer Faxt at US$200 a month for an individual and US$500 for a corporate seat in 2021 — a price that did not hold, and the database went free in February 2025; Bloomberg’s US$27,000–32,000 a seat, which Georgina Adam judged too high for the art trade (The Art Newspaper, 23 April 2026). The band is known.

## 5. How could we get a tech advantage on them?

Not by rebuilding the database. The record of every attempt says so: Artnet (1989–; taken private at €11.25 a share in August 2025 by Beowolff Capital, merged with Artsy in April 2026 with ‘dozens’ of layoffs, and now promising the ‘Bloomberg for the arts’ the trade doubts); Artprice (1997–; the largest database, €8.5m of revenue and €1,000 of net income in 2025); Mei Moses (the one independent repeat-sale index, taken inside Sotheby’s in 2016, so the market lost its neutral benchmark); Collectrium (built its database by scraping a competitor through rotating accounts, and was held liable — US$1,755,000 under the DMCA for circumventing access controls, though the listings’ copyright was ‘thin’ and the reuse fair use; arbitration award January 2019, confirmed in the Northern District of Texas in June 2019); Limna (valued a painting at €4,500 in a booth where the gallery asked three times that, on its first public test, 2021); Magnus (‘quietly folded’, The Art Newspaper, 2021); the Baer Faxt database (the one product that sold what the houses do not publish, free since 2025). The houses themselves run on bought data — Collectrium’s eleven million descriptions, Mei Moses, Thread Genius (a three-person unit) — and cut their own research (Christie’s closed its archive to the public in February 2021); their technology failures are on the record too (the May 2024 ransomware incident at Christie’s, more than 500,000 customers’ names and passport data exposed, a US$990,000 settlement approved in July 2025).

The advantage, then, is the thing nobody has built because it is work rather than code: the regulatory map end to end (export, import, VAT, sanctions, AML, protected material, cultural property — 32 jurisdictions in the Navigator, 33 playbooks, seven compliance files), the worked cases with the figures, the scorecard of the trade’s forecasters, the two-sided record of what the room saw, and an engine that composes them into a person’s case in a minute. The lawful data practice has two limits, not one. The Collectrium case (Christie’s v. Collectrium, S.D.N.Y. 2017) decided the copyright and computer-fraud points: the facts of a sale are free, and a login is a wall. The houses’ own website terms are the second wall: Sotheby’s terms (last modified 26 April 2019, read 16 November 2026) prohibit data mining, screen scraping and the harvesting of catalogue information by any automatic means and allow the content only for personal, non-commercial use, under New York law. So the terminal’s practice is manual reading and typing of published figures with the page cited (a research run opens a page and types the number; nothing crawls), the trade press and the houses’ press releases as the primary feed, open data under its licence (the National Gallery of Art, Cleveland, the Art Institute of Chicago, the government registers), and no image copied from a house. It names people only as the record names them (Defamation Act 2013, section 1: serious harm; the people rules mechanically enforced); it holds no client identity documents; it does not negotiate, lend or sell interests, so it is neither an art market participant nor an investment adviser.
