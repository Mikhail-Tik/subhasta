# Brief: the art-guessing games — Anthropeum and its kin, and the open collections a daily game can draw on

Read `BRIEF_COMMON.md` in this folder first; its rules on sources, flags and budget apply.

The owner found Anthropeum (a daily game: ten artefacts from The Met's open-access collection, a pin on a world map and a date on a timeline; made by Matthew Chu; Scientific American wrote it up) and wants Subhasta to carry a game of that kind — not a copy, something better and more gripping, for people who love this — and to connect it to the terminal's own data (the Hammer's prices, the dossiers, the movements) and to an educational section. Research two things.

## Part one: the games (`games`)

Every daily or competitive guessing game about art, artefacts, photographs or places that the record shows, with its mechanics in detail: Anthropeum (find its URL; how a round goes, the map, the timeline, how points are scored, tolerance, ten a day, streaks, sharing, archive, the data source and curation as the author describes it, press and user criticism), TimeGuessr (year + location of a photograph, scoring, daily and endless modes, the business model), Artle (the National Gallery of Art's Wordle for artists: four works, guesses, the share grid), Chronophoto, Framed/Wordle-likes for art if any, GeoGuessr itself (the scoring curve, the competitive modes, the subscription price and what it taught about retention), Backdrop, RelicGuessr, any 'guess the price' games (auction-price guessing, 'What's it worth', Antiques Roadshow quizzes), Google Arts & Culture's puzzles, museum-made games (the Met's, the Rijksmuseum's, the Getty's), the MutualArt or Artsy quizzes if any. For each: what the player does, the scoring formula where published, what makes it sticky (streaks, the share string, the daily limit, the leaderboards), what players complained about (fairness of the map, nondescript objects, the Eurocentric mix), the data it draws on and the licence, the business model if any. Then a section `design`: from the record, what would make a game of this kind better and more gripping — the mechanics the sources praise and the ones they damn — and, for the art trade in particular, the skills a game could test that no existing game does (estimating a hammer price, attributing a hand, placing a work in a movement, reading a condition, spotting a forgery pattern, recognising a provenance gap), with the sources that describe those skills as the trade's.

## Part two: the open collections (`collections`)

Every open-access museum collection API or data dump a browser page could draw a daily picture from, lawfully, with: the endpoint, whether it answers cross-origin requests from a browser (CORS; test it with a fetch if you can, otherwise say what the docs state), whether a key is needed and on what terms, the rate limits, the licence of the images and the metadata (CC0, public domain, the museum's own terms), which fields exist (date, date range, place of origin, culture, artist, medium, image URL, IIIF), the share of records with a public-domain image, and the terms-of-use page. Cover at least: The Met (collectionapi.metmuseum.org), the Art Institute of Chicago (api.artic.edu, IIIF), the Cleveland Museum of Art (openaccess-api.clevelandart.org), the Rijksmuseum (its API and the 2024–25 changes), Harvard Art Museums, the Smithsonian Open Access API, the National Gallery of Art's open data (GitHub), Europeana, the Getty's open content and the Getty Provenance Index, Wikidata/Wikimedia Commons (the SPARQL endpoint and the Commons API), the Walters, the Yale Center for British Art, the Paris Musées API, the Finnish National Gallery, the Nationalmuseum Stockholm, the Statens Museum for Kunst, the Victoria and Albert Museum API, the British Museum (what it allows), the Louvre (what it allows), the Hermitage (what it allows; and the sanctions question on using a Russian state museum's images — state what the sources say, if anything). For each, the exact URL of the docs page and of the terms, and a sample query that returns a dated, placed, public-domain painting.
